1. The strongest idea I see: KAN → symbolic chromatic primitives

This is the direction I would investigate first.

Your CNN gives you something like:

$$ P_k(\lambda)=\sum_i w_{ki}x_i $$

so you can plot the learned weights and interpret them as an SRF.

But KAN is fundamentally different: it learns functions, rather than just fixed scalar weights. The original KAN paper explicitly describes KANs as learnable univariate functions on edges and emphasizes their interpretability and ability to help discover mathematical relationships.

That gives you a very interesting research question:

Instead of only asking:

"What does the learned chromatic curve look like?"

ask:

"Can the learned chromatic primitive itself be represented by a compact mathematical function?"

For example, after training, you could try to approximate the learned primitive using functions such as:

$$ a e^{-\frac{(\lambda-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} $$

or combinations such as

$$ P(\lambda)= a_1G(\lambda;\mu_1,\sigma_1) +a_2G(\lambda;\mu_2,\sigma_2) +\cdots $$

or other candidate functions.

This is much more interesting than simply plotting the spline coefficients.

There is already active research using KANs for symbolic regression and scientific model discovery. For example, recent work explicitly uses KANs as a bridge between neural prediction and interpretable mathematical expressions.

And there is even a 2026 framework called SR-KAN specifically combining KANs with symbolic regression.

Your potential contribution would be:

Hyperspectral → KAN → learned chromatic primitive → symbolic SRF

That is a much stronger story.

4. Physics-inspired SRF constraints — but don't overdo it

This is another direction I like for your project.

Your objective isn't ordinary HSI classification. You're trying to learn something that behaves like a spectral sensitivity function.

So you can ask:

What properties should a useful artificial chromatic sensitivity function have?

For example:

Smoothness

Penalize violent band-to-band oscillation:

$$ L_{smooth} = \sum_\lambda (P(\lambda+1)-P(\lambda))^2 $$
Separation

Encourage different primitives to occupy different spectral regions.

Diversity

Prevent three primitives from becoming identical.

Optional physical feasibility

If you want the resulting curves to represent actual optical sensitivities, you could constrain them to be non-negative and bounded.

But this last point is important:

Don't force non-negativity just because CIE curves look positive.

Your current artificial vision formulation may produce opponent-like / discriminative responses, where negative values can have meaning. Your earlier observation about negative CNN curves is therefore not automatically an error.

So I would investigate the physics rather than blindly impose:

ReLU()

or clipping.

3. What exactly are we adding to your existing KAN?

Your current primitive is approximately:

$$ P_k(x) = \sum_b \phi_{k,b}(x_b) + \text{base contribution} $$

where:

\(x_b\) = reflectance/intensity at wavelength band \(b\)
\(k\) = primitive
\(\phi_{k,b}\) = learned KAN function for that spectral input.

This is fundamentally different from your CNN:

$$ P_k(x)=\sum_b w_{k,b}x_b $$

In CNN:

$$ \frac{\partial P_k}{\partial x_b}=w_{k,b} $$

So the 66 CNN weights directly give you a spectral curve.

For KAN, you have a function for each band, not one scalar weight.

That is exactly what we exploit.

4. Step 1 — Extract a spectral sensitivity curve from KAN

For each primitive \(k\), each wavelength \(b\), you have:

$$ \phi_{k,b}(x_b) $$

We can evaluate the learned function over the range of actual spectral values observed in the dataset.

Then calculate something like:

$$ S_{k,b} = E_x \left[ \left| \frac{\partial \phi_{k,b}(x_b)} {\partial x_b} \right| \right] $$

This gives:

$$ S_{k,b} $$

for every wavelength.

So finally:

Wavelength

 ▼     ▼     ▼           ▼     ▼

Primitive 1 → spectral sensitivity curve

5. Why this is stronger than the curves we were trying earlier

Previously, we were looking at:

base_linear.weight

spline_linear.weight

This is consistent with why KAN is attractive for interpretability in the first place. Recent KAN model-discovery work explicitly uses the learned univariate functions to understand relationships between inputs and outputs.

6. Step 2 — Make the primitives complementary

Now suppose your three primitives learn:

P1 → 500–600 nm
P2 → 550–700 nm
P3 → 500–650 nm

They may all be learning almost the same thing.

That isn't ideal.

You want something more like:

P1 → blue/green region
P2 → green/yellow region
P3 → red/NIR region

Not necessarily RGB-like — data should decide the regions.

So introduce a diversity loss:

$$ L = L_{classification} + \lambda_d L_{diversity} + \lambda_s L_{smooth} $$

For example:

$$ L_{diversity} = \sum_{i\neq j} |\operatorname{corr}(S_i,S_j)| $$

This discourages highly correlated spectral sensitivities.

7. Step 3 — Smoothness

A real spectral sensitivity curve shouldn't normally look like:

_/\/\_/\/\/\__/\/\_

unless there is a genuine reason.

So add:

$$ L_{smooth} = \sum_b (S_{b+1}-S_b)^2 $$

This encourages smooth spectral responses.

This is also well motivated by current camera spectral-sensitivity research: recent work explicitly discusses the problem of learned sensitivity curves becoming irregular/jagged and introduces losses for smooth, physically feasible sensitivity curves.

JOCSR: Joint optimization of camera spectral sensitivity and spectral reconstruction

So this gives you a nice connection:

KAN interpretability + artificial color + physically motivated spectral smoothness

There is a classical and still very relevant concept called Goodness of Pronunciation (GOP).

The basic idea is:

Given an expected phoneme, how strongly does the acoustic model believe that the student actually produced that phoneme?

Student audio segment
        ↓

        ↓

P(/θ/ | audio) = 0.12
P(/t/ | audio) = 0.72

Result:
/θ/ → /t/

Mispronunciation detected

/t r iː/

Substitution

GOP traditionally uses phoneme alignment and acoustic-model probabilities. More recent work is also exploring CTC-based and alignment-free variants, including phonological knowledge and restricted likely substitutions.

1. First: what exactly are we trying to detect?

Let's define the problem mathematically.

Suppose the teacher says a sentence:

“The three boys are playing.”

We have:

$$ A_T = \text{teacher audio} $$

and the student says the same sentence:

$$ A_S = \text{student audio} $$

We also know the intended text:

$$ T = \text{"The three boys are playing"} $$

We want:

$$ f(A_T,A_S,T,L) \rightarrow \{\text{errors, locations, types, scores, explanations}\} $$

where \(L\) is the language.

The output shouldn't simply be:

Pronunciation score = 72%

It should ideally be:

Sentence: The three boys are playing.

Overall score: 82/100

Word: three
    phoneme: /θ/
    expected: /θ/
    detected: /t/
    error: substitution
    confidence: 0.91
    time: 1.42–1.61 s

Word: playing
    phoneme: /eɪ/
    expected duration: 210 ms
    student duration: 91 ms
    error: vowel reduction

And eventually:

You pronounced /θ/ closer to /t/. Try placing the tongue between the teeth rather than behind the teeth.

That last part is where this becomes a real AI pronunciation tutor, rather than an audio-comparison project.

2. The biggest conceptual mistake to avoid

You mentioned:

amplitude, frequency, where they differ

Those are useful, but raw amplitude/frequency are not good representations for pronunciation comparison.

Consider:

Teacher:
male, 30 years old, loud voice

Student:
female, 15 years old, quiet voice

Their waveforms will look very different even if both pronounce the word perfectly.

Similarly:

Teacher pitch = 120 Hz
Student pitch = 220 Hz

does not mean the student mispronounced the word.

So:

Don't compare:
$$ waveform_T(t) \leftrightarrow waveform_S(t) $$

directly.

Instead compare representations that encode phonetic information.

Meta's MMS goes even further: its pretrained speech models cover more than 1,000 languages, with an ASR model covering 1,107 languages.

So for your project, a much better idea is:

Use a multilingual self-supervised speech representation as the common acoustic space rather than creating a separate acoustic model from scratch for every language.

       │                 │
       │          /u/    │
       │                 │
       │      /a/        │
       └─────────────────┘
                F1 →

If the student produces the wrong vowel, the formant trajectory can differ.

This gives you a physically interpretable feature, unlike a black-box embedding.

10. Pitch / F0

Fundamental frequency:

$$ F_0 = \frac{1}{T_0} $$

where \(T_0\) is the glottal period.

Useful for:

stress
intonation
tone
emphasis
prosody

This becomes very important for multilingual speech.

For example, in Mandarin, pitch can distinguish lexical meaning.

So:

same approximate phoneme sequence
+
different F0 contour

may actually represent a pronunciation/tone error.

For a truly multilingual system, you should not ignore F0.

If the text is known:

     ↓

     ↓
Alignment model
     ↓

You get:

--------------------------------

This is extremely useful.

Montreal Forced Aligner (MFA) is one established tool for this. It takes orthographic transcription and a pronunciation dictionary and generates time-aligned phones; its ecosystem has pretrained models for many languages.

Current MFA documentation also includes language-specific processing for languages such as Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Thai, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, etc.

But don't make MFA your research contribution.

Treat it as infrastructure/baseline.

So different speaking speeds can be aligned.

17. Now we get to the actual mispronunciation detector

Suppose after alignment we have:

Teacher phoneme: /θ/
Student segment: corresponding audio

There are several ways to determine whether it is correct.

Method 1 — Acoustic distance

Calculate:

$$ D = distance(F_T,F_S) $$

where \(F\) could be MFCC, Mel features, embeddings, etc.

If:

$$ D > \tau $$

then flag it.

Problem

A different speaker naturally produces different features.

So simple distance can give false positives.

18. Method 2 — GOP

This is a much more principled pronunciation-assessment approach.

For expected phoneme \(p\):

$$ GOP(p)= \log P(p|O) - \frac{1}{N}\max_q \log P(q|O) $$

Conceptually:

Expected phoneme
       ↓
How probable is this phoneme
given the student's acoustic signal?

Suppose:

Expected = /θ/

P(θ | audio) = 0.08
P(t | audio) = 0.78
P(s | audio) = 0.10

Then:

Likely substitution:

θ → t

This is far more meaningful than:

frequency difference = 17%

SpeechOcean762 is especially valuable because its annotations explicitly include phoneme-level pronunciation scores and, for low-scoring phones, likely mispronunciations such as canonical L → pronounced D.

19. Method 3 — Phoneme posterior comparison

Instead of asking only:

Is the expected phoneme present?

generate a probability distribution:

Student segment

θ    0.08
t    0.72
d    0.06
s    0.10
...

Then:

Expected = θ
Top predicted = t

→ substitution θ → t

This gives you an extremely useful output.

20. Method 4 — Embedding similarity

Take teacher and student phoneme segments:

Teacher /θ/
       ↓
Speech encoder
       ↓
embedding T

and:

Student /θ/
       ↓
Speech encoder
       ↓
embedding S

Then:

$$ similarity = \frac{T\cdot S}{||T||||S||} $$

or use a learned distance.

This gives:

Correct pronunciation:
similarity = 0.92

Incorrect:
similarity = 0.41

But there is a problem:

The teacher and student may have different speakers.

So you need the model to learn:

speaker difference should be ignored; phonetic difference should matter.
